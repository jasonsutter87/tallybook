When you work for an employer, tax comes out of every paycheck. When you work
for yourself, nobody withholds anything, so the IRS expects you to pay as you
go: four times a year, in **estimated tax payments**.

Miss them, or pay too little, and you owe an **underpayment penalty**. It works
like interest on what you should have paid, and it adds up quietly. The good
news is that one rule, the **safe harbor**, makes the whole thing predictable.

## Do you need to pay estimated taxes?

Generally, yes, if you expect to owe **$1,000 or more** in federal tax for the
year after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. For most freelancers,
consultants and sole proprietors with a profitable year, that's you.

What you're paying toward is two taxes:

- **Income tax** on your profit, at your normal brackets.
- **Self-employment tax**, the Social Security and Medicare that an employer
  would otherwise split with you. It's 15.3% of 92.35% of your net earnings:
  12.4% for Social Security (up to the year's wage base, **$184,500 for 2026**)
  and 2.9% for Medicare, with no cap.

That second one surprises people. A $60,000 profit carries roughly $8,500 of
self-employment tax before any income tax at all.

## When are the payments due?

For the 2026 tax year, the federal due dates are:

| Payment | Covers income earned | Due |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | January – March | April 15, 2026 |
| 2 | April – May | June 15, 2026 |
| 3 | June – August | September 15, 2026 |
| 4 | September – December | January 15, 2027 |

When a date falls on a weekend or holiday, it moves to the next business day.
Notice the periods aren't equal: the second payment comes only two months after
the first.

## The safe-harbor rule

Here's the part that takes the stress out of it. You **avoid the federal
underpayment penalty** if your four payments add up to at least the smaller of:

1. **100% of last year's total tax** (from your prior-year return), or
2. **90% of this year's tax**.

If last year's adjusted gross income was **over $150,000** ($75,000 if married
filing separately), the first number becomes **110%** of last year's tax.

The first option is the one to love. Last year's tax is a number you already
know, sitting on line 24 of last year's Form 1040. It doesn't move when this
year's income does. Pay a quarter of it each due date and you're covered, even
if this turns out to be your best year ever.

> **Example.** Last year your total tax was $12,000 and your AGI was $95,000.
> Your safe harbor is $12,000, so you pay **$3,000 each quarter**. If your AGI
> had been $180,000, it would be 110% of $12,000, or $13,200: **$3,300 a
> quarter**.

You'll still owe whatever the real tax turns out to be when you file in April.
Safe harbor protects you from the *penalty*, not from the bill. So if your
income is climbing, set money aside beyond the safe-harbor payments, too.

### When the 90% option is better

If this year is clearly worse than last, say you took months off or lost a big
client, paying 90% of this year's projected tax may mean smaller payments. The
catch is that you have to estimate this year's profit, and if you guess low,
the protection goes away. It's the right tool when income has dropped and you
can project it with some confidence.

### First year on your own?

If you had no tax liability last year (and the year was a full 12 months), you
generally don't owe an underpayment penalty for this one. That's worth
confirming against the IRS instructions for your situation, and it's still wise
to set money aside, because the full bill arrives in April either way.

## How to pay

The simplest ways are **IRS Direct Pay** (from a bank account, no sign-up),
your **IRS Online Account**, or **EFTPS**. Choose "estimated tax" and the right
tax year. Keep the confirmation numbers; you'll report the total on your return.

Most states with an income tax have their own estimated payments, with their own
rules and often the same due dates. Check your state's revenue department.

## How much to set aside from each payment you receive

A rule of thumb many freelancers use is to move **25–30% of every payment** into
a separate savings account the day it arrives. Pay your quarterly estimates from
that account. Whatever's left at filing time covers the difference, or becomes a
pleasant surprise.

## The bookkeeping side

Estimated income tax is a personal tax, not a business expense. In your books,
record each payment as an **owner's draw** (money you took out of the business),
not as a deduction on Schedule C. Keep a list of what you paid and when; your
preparer will ask for it.
