It's the 3rd of the month. Rent went out on the 1st, the car insurance on the
2nd, and your budget app is a wall of red. You haven't done anything wrong. The
app just doesn't know what day it is.

Most budgets compare *what you've spent so far* with *the whole month's plan*.
That works on the 31st. On every other day, it's either a false alarm or a false
comfort. A budget that tells you the truth needs one more ingredient: **pace**.

## Step 1: Know where the month is

Pace means asking a different question. Not "have I spent less than $900 on
groceries?" but "**at the rate I'm going, will I end the month under $900?**"

On the 10th, a third of the month is gone. If you've spent $330 of a $900
grocery plan, you're right on pace, even though a third of your money is gone.
If you've spent $500, you're running hot, and it's the 10th, so there's time to
adjust.

## Step 2: Not every line should pace the same way

Pace works for spending that happens a little at a time. It's wrong for spending
that happens all at once. So sort your lines into three kinds:

- **Fixed** — the same amount, on a known date: rent, insurance, loan payments,
  subscriptions. Don't pace these. They're either paid or they're not.
- **Moves with use** — groceries, gas, power, water. Pace these: they happen
  through the month and you have some control.
- **Your choice** — dining out, coffee, shopping, fun. Pace these too, and these
  are where a tight month actually bends.

That third group is the useful one. When money is short, it's rarely the rent
you can change. Seeing how much of the month sits in "your choice" tells you how
much room you really have.

## Step 3: Some lines are floors, not ceilings

Most budget lines are ceilings: *spend at most* this much. But some goals point
the other way:

- **Savings**: put away *at least* $600 this month.
- **Income**: bring in *at least* what the plan assumes.
- **Debt payoff**: pay *at least* the extra $200.

Treating these as ceilings gets them backwards: hitting $700 in savings shouldn't
show up as "over budget." Mark them as floors and they turn green when you pass
them.

## Step 4: Budget by person, too

In a household, "where did the money go?" often really means "**who** was it
for?" The kids' soccer fees, new school clothes, a partner's commute. A plan line
per person, alongside the category lines, answers the question families actually
argue about, without anyone keeping receipts.

## Step 5: Let bills tick themselves off

A list of bills with due dates is the other half of a calm month. The trick is
not to mark them paid by hand. When the payment shows up from the bank, the bill
should tick itself off; when it doesn't show up a few days after its due date,
that's worth a heads-up. Everything else stays quiet.

## Step 6: Carry the plan forward

Re-entering a budget every month is how budgets die. Set the plan once; let each
new month start from last month's; change only what changed. Your future self
will keep using a budget that takes two minutes, not twenty.
