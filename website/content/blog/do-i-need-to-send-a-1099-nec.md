If you pay a freelancer, a designer, a bookkeeper or any other independent
contractor, the IRS may want to hear about it, on **Form 1099-NEC**. Here's who
needs one, the threshold that changed for 2026, and the habit that makes January
painless.

## The short version

You generally need to file a 1099-NEC for a person or business when **all** of
these are true:

1. You paid them **for services** (not for goods or merchandise).
2. You paid them **in the course of your trade or business** (not a personal
   payment, like a babysitter for your own kids).
3. They're **not a corporation** (with exceptions, like attorneys).
4. You paid them **at least the threshold** during the year, by a method that
   counts (more on that below).

## The threshold: $600 becomes $2,000

For decades the threshold was **$600**. Starting with **payments made in 2026**,
it rises to **$2,000**, under Public Law 119-21. It's set to adjust for inflation
in later years.

| Payments made in | Threshold |
|---|---|
| 2025 | $600 |
| 2026 | $2,000 |

So a contractor you paid $1,500 in 2026 no longer needs a 1099-NEC from you. The
threshold applies per payee, across the whole year: four $600 payments to the
same designer add up to $2,400, which is over it.

## Card and PayPal payments don't count

This is the rule that saves the most paperwork. If you paid a contractor by
**credit card, debit card, or a payment app or processor** like PayPal or Stripe,
you **don't** include those payments on a 1099-NEC. The payment network reports
them to the IRS on **Form 1099-K** instead, and counting them on both forms would
double-report the income.

What you report are payments by **check, bank transfer (ACH), wire, or cash**.
That means you need your books to know *how* each contractor was paid, not just
how much.

> **Example.** You paid a developer $3,000 in 2026: $2,500 by ACH and $500 on
> your business credit card. Only the $2,500 counts toward the threshold. It's
> over $2,000, so you file a 1099-NEC for **$2,500**.

## Collect a W-9 before the first payment

Before you pay a contractor, ask them for a **Form W-9**. It gives you their legal
name, business type and taxpayer ID (SSN or EIN), which you need to file the
1099-NEC. Chasing W-9s in January, from people you haven't worked with since
spring, is the worst part of the whole process. Asking up front takes a minute.

Keep W-9s somewhere safe. They contain sensitive numbers and don't belong in a
shared folder or a spreadsheet emailed around.

## When it's due

The 1099-NEC is due **January 31** of the following year, both to the contractor
and to the IRS (on paper, or electronically through the IRS's free IRIS portal or
your filing software). When January 31 falls on a weekend, it moves to the next
business day. There's no automatic extension, so don't leave it for the last day.

## The bookkeeping habit that makes it easy

Two small habits turn 1099 season into a ten-minute job:

- **Tag every contractor payment with the payee.** "Contract labor" as a category
  tells you the total, but not who it went to.
- **Record how you paid.** Bank or card makes the difference between "needs a
  1099" and "doesn't."
