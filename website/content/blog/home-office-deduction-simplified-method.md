If you work for yourself from home, part of your home is doing business, and
the IRS lets you deduct it. There are two ways to work out how much: a quick
one that fits on a sticky note, and a thorough one that fits on a whole form.
Here's how to pick.

## First: does your space count?

The deduction isn't for "I sometimes answer email on the couch." The space has
to be used:

- **Regularly.** It's where the work actually happens, not once in a while.
- **Exclusively.** For business, and nothing else. A spare room that's your
  office counts. The dining table where you also eat dinner doesn't, and
  neither does the guest room with a desk in the corner.
- **As your principal place of business**, or as a place you regularly meet
  clients, or as a separate structure like a detached studio. If you do your
  admin at home and your real work on job sites, the admin usually counts.

There are a couple of exceptions to the exclusive-use rule, such as storing
inventory or running a daycare, but for most freelancers the test is simple:
**could you point at the space and say "that's the office"?**

One more catch: this is for the **self-employed**. Under current law, W-2
employees can't take a home office deduction on their federal return, even if
the boss sent everyone home.

## The quick way: the simplified method

Multiply your office's square footage by **$5**, up to **300 square feet**.

| Office size | Deduction |
|---|---|
| 100 sq ft (a small bedroom) | $500 |
| 200 sq ft | $1,000 |
| 300 sq ft or more | $1,500, the cap |

That's it. No utility bills to add up, no depreciation, nothing to carry
forward. A few rules come with it:

- **It can't create a loss.** The deduction is limited to your business's
  profit, and with this method anything over the limit is simply lost, not
  carried to next year.
- **Mortgage interest and property tax stay whole.** If you itemize, you
  deduct them in full on Schedule A instead of splitting them with the office.
- **No depreciation means no recapture.** When you sell your home, there's no
  depreciation to pay tax on later. That's a quiet advantage people miss.

## The long way: the regular method (Form 8829)

Work out what share of your home the office is, and deduct that share of the
real costs.

1. **Business percentage:** office square feet ÷ home square feet. A 200 sq ft
   office in a 1,600 sq ft home is **12.5%**.
2. **Indirect expenses** (the whole home): rent or mortgage interest, property
   tax, homeowner's or renter's insurance, utilities, general repairs, and
   depreciation if you own. You deduct the business percentage of these.
3. **Direct expenses** (the office only), like painting that one room: deduct
   all of it.

Unlike the simplified method, anything over your profit limit **carries
forward** to next year.

## Which one pays more?

Run a quick example. You rent a 1,600 sq ft apartment for $2,000 a month,
utilities and renter's insurance add $300 a month, and your office is 200 sq ft.

- **Simplified:** 200 × $5 = **$1,000**.
- **Regular:** 12.5% × ($24,000 rent + $3,600 other) = **$3,450**.

In an expensive rental, the regular method can win by a lot. It tends to lose
when housing is cheap, when your office is small relative to your home, or when
you'd rather not keep (and defend) a folder of utility bills. Homeowners should
also weigh the depreciation recapture they'd owe on a later sale.

**You can switch methods each year**, so you're not locked in. Some people run
both numbers every spring and take the bigger one.

## The paperwork that saves you

Whichever method you use, keep:

- a floor plan or a quick sketch with measurements,
- a couple of photos of the space set up as an office, and
- for the regular method, the year's rent or mortgage, insurance and utility
  statements.

That's the whole file if the IRS ever asks.
