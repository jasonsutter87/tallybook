It happens to everyone. You're at the store, you grab the wrong card, and
now your business paid for a week of groceries. Or a client paid you into
your personal account, and that money sits next to your rent.

Accountants call this **commingling**, and they say it the way dentists say
"flossing." Here's why it matters, how to stop doing it, and what to do on
the days you do it anyway.

## Why keep them apart?

**Your taxes get easier.** When the business has its own account, every
transaction in it is probably a business transaction. When everything shares
one account, you have to make that call on every coffee, every Amazon
order and every transfer, every year.

**Your deductions are easier to defend.** If the IRS ever asks about an
expense, "here's the business account statement" is a much better answer
than "I'm pretty sure that one was for work."

**You can see if the business is working.** Mixed money hides the most
important number you have: what the business actually earns.

**If you have an LLC, it helps protect you.** Part of what keeps business
debts away from your personal assets is that the two are really separate.
Treating the business account like a personal piggy bank can weaken that.

## The setup: two accounts and a habit

1. **Open a separate checking account for the business**, and if you use a
   card, a separate card too. It doesn't need to be a fancy business account.
   For a sole proprietor, a second personal account used only for business
   works.
2. **All business money goes in and out of it.** Client payments in, business
   expenses out.
3. **Pay yourself on purpose.** Move money from the business account to your
   personal account, on a schedule if you can: every two weeks, or monthly.

That last step has a name.

## Paying yourself: the owner's draw

If you're a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC, you don't put yourself on
payroll. You take an **owner's draw**: a transfer from the business to you.

A draw is **not an expense**. It doesn't lower your business profit, and it
isn't taxed when you take it. You're taxed on the business's profit whether
you leave the money in the business or draw every penny. The draw is just
moving your own money from one pocket to the other.

The reverse is an **owner's contribution**: putting your own money into the
business, like covering a big purchase from savings.

(Corporations and S corps work differently, with salaries and payroll taxes.
If that's you, ask your accountant.)

## When you mix them anyway

You will. Here's the fix, in plain bookkeeping:

**Business card, personal purchase (the groceries).** Record it as an
**owner's draw**. The business didn't buy groceries; it gave you money, and
you spent it on groceries. Not deductible, no harm done.

**Personal card, business purchase (the printer ink).** Record it as a
**business expense paid with an owner's contribution**. You put your own
money into the business, which then bought ink. Still deductible.

**Client paid your personal account.** Record the income in the business
anyway (it's still business income), paid with an owner's draw, because the
money went straight to you. Then remind the client where to pay next time.

None of these needs fixing at the bank. You don't have to move the money
back. You just have to **record what really happened**, so your profit is
right.
